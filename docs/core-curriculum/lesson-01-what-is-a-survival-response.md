# WHAT IS A SURVIVAL RESPONSE?

### 1. RECOGNIZE

**Have you ever...**

walked into a room and immediately noticed who was there?

Before anyone said a word, you already knew where the exits were.

You noticed the person who looked angry.

You noticed the person who looked safe.

You noticed the tone of someone's voice.

Maybe nothing was actually happening.

But some part of you was already preparing.

Or maybe you've had a completely different version.

Someone says, *"We need to talk."*

Your stomach drops.

Your shoulders tighten.

Your mind starts running through everything that could go wrong.

You haven't even heard what they want yet.

Your body is already getting ready.

Sometimes that reaction helps.

Sometimes it shows up when you don't need it anymore.

That's the important part.

**Your body can learn to prepare for danger.**

And once it learns that lesson, it may keep doing it.

---

### 2. NAME

There is a simple name for this:

## **A survival response.**

A survival response is what happens when your body and mind get ready to protect you.

It can happen when danger is real.

It can also happen when something **feels** dangerous because it reminds you of something your system has learned to watch for.

---

### 3. EXPLAIN

Your body is built to protect you.

It does not always wait for you to sit down and think through every detail.

If something looks dangerous, your body can move first.

Your heart may speed up.

Your muscles may tighten.

You may become very alert.

You may want to leave.

You may become very quiet.

You may get angry.

You may feel like you need to do something **right now**.

That doesn't mean you're broken.

It means your protection system is doing what it was built to do.

The problem comes when the alarm goes off in situations where you need something different.

A meeting is not a fight.

A difficult phone call is not a physical attack.

Someone being disappointed with you does not automatically mean you're in danger.

But your body may not always make that distinction quickly.

---

### 4. SEPARATE

Understanding a survival response does **not** mean everything you do during one is okay.

You are still responsible for what you do.

If you hurt somebody, that matters.

If you break a promise, that matters.

If you avoid something until it becomes worse, that matters.

Knowing what may have been happening inside you gives you **more information**.

It does not take away your choices.

The goal isn't:

> "I can't help it."

The goal is:

> **"Now I can recognize what's happening and choose what I do next."**

---

### 5. INTERRUPT

When you notice your system gearing up, **stop and look around.**

Don't solve the whole problem.

Just answer three questions:

**Where am I?**

**What is actually happening right now?**

**What do I need to do next?**

That's it.

Bring yourself back to the room you're actually in.

Not yesterday.

Not ten minutes from now.

**Right now.**

---

### 6. PRACTICE

You receive a text:

> **"Call me when you get a chance."**

Nothing else.

You feel your body tighten.

Your first thought is:

*"What did I do?"*

You want to ignore it.

Run the scene in your head.

Don't explain yourself.

Don't tell anyone what the text reminds you of.

Just ask:

**What is actually happening?**

The only fact you have is:

> Someone asked you to call.

Now choose the smallest next action.

Maybe you call.

Maybe you wait ten minutes and then call.

Maybe you send:

> *"I'll call you this afternoon."*

The point isn't which choice you make.

The point is noticing the difference between **what happened** and **what your alarm predicted.**

---

### 7. CARRY IT

> **Your alarm can be real even when the danger isn't.**
