# UNDERSTANDING DISSOCIATION

### 1. RECOGNIZE

**Have you ever...**

driven somewhere and suddenly realized you don't remember much of the drive?

You know you got there.

You remember leaving.

But the middle is fuzzy.

Or maybe someone is talking to you and you can hear every word, but it feels like you're several feet away from the conversation.

You answer.

You nod.

You look like you're there.

But inside, everything feels far away.

Or maybe you've been sitting somewhere for a long time and suddenly realize you haven't really been paying attention to anything around you.

You look at the clock.

An hour passed.

You barely noticed.

Sometimes people describe this as:

> *"I checked out."*

That's a useful phrase.

Because sometimes the mind creates distance when what's happening feels like too much to stay fully engaged with.

---

### 2. NAME

One name for this experience is:

## **Dissociation.**

In plain language:

**You are here, but part of your attention has pulled away.**

It can be mild.

It can last a short time.

It can happen when you're stressed, overwhelmed, exhausted, or deeply focused.

It doesn't automatically mean something is seriously wrong.

The important thing is learning to recognize when it's happening.

---

### 3. EXPLAIN

Your attention is not a light switch.

It can move.

It can narrow.

It can drift.

Sometimes the mind creates distance from what is happening around you.

That can make it harder to:

* follow a conversation
* remember what was just said
* make a decision
* notice what's happening around you
* stay connected to what you are doing

You may not choose for it to happen.

But once you notice it, you can begin finding your way back.

---

### 4. SEPARATE

Understanding dissociation doesn't mean ignoring responsibilities.

If you miss something because you checked out, the missed thing still matters.

If someone is talking to you and you didn't hear them, you can ask them to repeat themselves.

If you're driving and realize you aren't fully focused, **safety comes first.**

The point isn't:

> "I can't help it."

The point is:

> **"I can learn to notice when I'm slipping away and practice coming back."**

---

### 5. INTERRUPT

Use your surroundings.

## **Name five things you can see.**

Then notice:

**Where are my feet?**

**What am I holding?**

**What can I hear?**

**What am I doing right now?**

You aren't trying to force yourself to feel anything.

You're simply giving your attention something concrete to return to.

---

### 6. PRACTICE

Imagine you're in a meeting.

Someone has been talking for several minutes.

You realize you haven't followed the conversation.

Don't tell anyone what you're thinking.

Just run the exercise silently:

**Five things I can see.**

**My feet are on the floor.**

**I can hear the speaker.**

**I am sitting in this room.**

**What are they talking about right now?**

Then identify **one fact** from the conversation.

That's your anchor.

You don't need to explain where your attention went.

You don't need to tell anyone your history.

You just practice coming back.

---

### 7. CARRY IT

> **When you notice yourself checking out, come back to what is real and happening right now.**
