# Core Lesson 1 — What Is a Survival Response?

## Corrections Track

### 1. Core Connection

A person can react to perceived threat before they have fully processed what is happening.

The body may become tense.

Attention may narrow.

The person may become angry, defensive, silent, or ready to leave.

Recognizing the response creates a chance to interrupt it.

### 2. Environmental Translation

Corrections environments can contain authority, rules, uncertainty, confrontation, limited control, and constant awareness of consequences.

A participant may interpret an interaction as threatening before knowing exactly what is happening.

The response may then become part of the problem.

The goal is not to label the reaction as bad.

The goal is to recognize it early enough to make a different choice.

### 3. Recognizable Scenario

A staff member tells a participant:

> “I need to talk with you.”

The participant immediately becomes tense.

Their heart begins racing.

They assume they are about to receive another consequence.

Before the staff member explains why, the participant becomes argumentative.

### 4. Seven-Step Application

**Recognize**

Notice the physical and behavioral signs:

* tension
* rapid heartbeat
* narrowed attention
* anger
* urge to argue
* urge to leave
* shutting down

**Name**

> “My body is reacting as if something bad is about to happen.”

**Explain**

The body can respond to perceived threat before all the facts are known.

That may explain the reaction.

It does not determine what the participant does next.

**Separate**

Separate:

* perceived threat from confirmed threat
* automatic response from chosen behavior
* explanation from excuse
* another person's action from my response

**Interrupt**

Ask:

> **“What do I actually know right now?”**

Then slow the response enough to gather information.

**Practice**

Use a neutral response:

> “Okay. What is this about?”

Listen before deciding what the interaction means.

**Carry It**

> **“Recognize the response before letting it run the interaction.”**

### 5. Accountability Check

> **What part belongs to me?**

Recognizing my reaction and managing my behavior once I have enough awareness to do so.

### 6. Boundary Check

> **What part does not belong to me?**

I do not control another person's actions, tone, decisions, or intentions.

### 7. Next Action

When I notice my body escalating, pause long enough to identify what I actually know before responding.

### 8. Carry-Forward

> **Notice the response. Get the facts. Choose the next move.**

## Reentry Track

### 1. Core Connection

A survival response can continue showing up even when the immediate environment has changed.

Recognizing the response creates room for a different choice.

### 2. Environmental Translation

Reentry can involve uncertainty, rejection, unfamiliar expectations, financial pressure, employment, housing, family conflict, and responsibility.

A person may experience an ordinary setback as an immediate threat.

The body reacts.

The person may then avoid the situation, become angry, shut down, or give up.

### 3. Recognizable Scenario

A person receives an email saying:

> “We need additional information before we can process your application.”

The person immediately assumes they have been rejected.

They become angry and stop responding.

The actual message did not say they were rejected.

### 4. Seven-Step Application

**Recognize**

Notice the shift:

> “Something happened, and my body immediately went into threat mode.”

**Name**

> “I am reacting to uncertainty as if I already know the outcome.”

**Explain**

Uncertainty can feel threatening when a person has experienced repeated instability or rejection.

That explains the reaction.

It does not make the conclusion accurate.

**Separate**

Separate:

* the event
* the body's response
* the meaning I assigned to it
* the behavior I choose next

**Interrupt**

Ask:

> **“What actually happened?”**

Then:

> **“What do I know, and what am I predicting?”**

**Practice**

Respond to the actual message.

Provide the requested information.

Ask what is needed.

Do not make the feared outcome a fact before it happens.

**Carry It**

> **“A threat response is not a prediction of the future.”**

### 5. Accountability Check

> **What part belongs to me?**

How I respond to uncertainty and whether I allow the initial reaction to determine my behavior.

### 6. Boundary Check

> **What part does not belong to me?**

Another person's decision, the timing of an institution, or circumstances outside my control.

### 7. Next Action

When uncertainty triggers a strong reaction, identify the actual event before deciding what it means.

### 8. Carry-Forward

> **Respond to what happened—not only to what you fear will happen.**

## Veteran Track

### 1. Core Connection

The body can respond quickly to perceived threat.

That response may have been useful in one environment and less useful in another.

Recognizing the response creates room for adaptation.

### 2. Environmental Translation

Military environments can require rapid attention to potential threats, quick decisions, readiness, and immediate action.

Civilian environments often contain ambiguity, slower processes, and situations where information needs to be gathered before acting.

A rapid threat response can therefore be misread by others—or create unnecessary conflict.

### 3. Recognizable Scenario

A person enters a crowded workplace meeting.

Someone moves quickly behind them.

They immediately turn, tense up, and respond sharply.

The other person was simply trying to get past them.

### 4. Seven-Step Application

**Recognize**

Notice the rapid physical shift:

* tension
* heightened attention
* scanning
* irritability
* immediate readiness to respond

**Name**

> “My attention just shifted into threat detection.”

**Explain**

Rapid awareness can be useful when a real threat exists.

It can also activate in situations that only resemble previous danger.

**Separate**

Separate:

* awareness from certainty
* readiness from actual danger
* automatic response from deliberate action
* current evidence from previous experience

**Interrupt**

Ask:

> **“What is actually happening right now?”**

**Practice**

Pause.

Look at the environment.

Identify what is actually occurring.

Then respond proportionately to the current situation.

**Carry It**

> **“Stay alert without treating every alert as an emergency.”**

### 5. Accountability Check

> **What part belongs to me?**

My behavior after recognizing that my body has shifted into a heightened state.

### 6. Boundary Check

> **What part does not belong to me?**

I cannot control every movement, noise, or action occurring around me.

### 7. Next Action

When I notice an immediate threat response, identify one piece of present evidence before acting.

### 8. Carry-Forward

> **Use awareness to gather information, not automatically to declare danger.**

## Workplace Track

### 1. Core Connection

Pressure can produce a rapid physical and behavioral response.

The response may happen before a person has fully considered what is actually happening.

Recognizing it creates an opportunity to choose a better response.

### 2. Environmental Translation

Workplaces contain deadlines, feedback, authority, mistakes, customer demands, conflict, and uncertainty.

An employee may experience ordinary correction as personal threat.

The resulting defensiveness can create a larger problem than the original issue.

### 3. Recognizable Scenario

A supervisor says:

> “Can we talk about yesterday's work?”

The employee immediately assumes they are being fired.

Their body tightens.

They become defensive before hearing the feedback.

The supervisor actually wants to discuss one correctable mistake.

### 4. Seven-Step Application

**Recognize**

Notice:

* tension
* racing thoughts
* urge to defend
* anger
* withdrawal
* shutting down
* immediate negative assumptions

**Name**

> “I am reacting to the possibility of criticism before I know what the conversation is about.”

**Explain**

Previous experiences can make criticism feel threatening.

That can explain why the reaction appears quickly.

It does not mean the current supervisor intends harm.

**Separate**

Separate:

* feedback from attack
* discomfort from danger
* automatic response from chosen behavior
* assumption from fact

**Interrupt**

Ask:

> **“What did the person actually say?”**

Then listen to the rest.

**Practice**

Respond:

> “Sure. What would you like to go over?”

Hear the feedback before defending yourself.

**Carry It**

> **“I can feel threatened without automatically treating the situation as a threat.”**

### 5. Accountability Check

> **What part belongs to me?**

My communication, behavior, and willingness to hear information before reacting.

### 6. Boundary Check

> **What part does not belong to me?**

Another person's tone, expectations, judgment, or decision.

### 7. Next Action

When feedback triggers a strong reaction, listen to the complete message before responding.

### 8. Carry-Forward

> **Hear the whole message before deciding what it means.**
