# PHASE 3 — FACILITATOR EXECUTION MATRIX

## Core Lessons 5–8

### Facilitator Operating Standard

The facilitator is not there to prove a participant wrong.

The job is to help the participant move from **automatic interpretation → accurate observation → personal responsibility → practical action**.

Across all four tracks, keep the same governing standard:

> **Understand the behavior. Tell the truth about the behavior. Own the behavior. Change what can be changed.**

No forced disclosure. No diagnostic language. No assumption that the participant's perception is automatically wrong. No assumption that the institution, other person, or participant is automatically right.

---

# CORE LESSON 5 — PERCEPTION VS. FACT

## 1. Facilitator Anchor

### Core Trap

The central trap is:

> **“Because I experienced it strongly, my interpretation must be a fact.”**

Common versions:

* “I know what they were thinking.”
* “I could tell they were lying.”
* “They were obviously trying to disrespect me.”
* “I've been through this before.”
* “My gut doesn't lie.”

The opposite trap is equally important:

> **“If I cannot prove it, my perception means nothing.”**

Neither is the standard.

### Facilitator Reframe

> **“Your perception is information. It is not automatically proof.”**

The facilitator should not tell participants to ignore intuition.

Instead, add verification:

> **“Notice it. Name it. Check it.”**

### Anchor Line

> **“What you felt is real. What you think it means still needs to be checked.”**

### Common Pushback

**Participant:**

> “You weren't there. You don't know.”

**Facilitator:**

> “Correct. I'm not asking you to accept my interpretation. I'm asking you to separate what you know from what you concluded.”

That response is important because it avoids claiming authority over the participant's experience.

---

## 2. Room Dynamics & De-escalation

### Pushback: “I know what they meant.”

> “You may be right. What did they actually say or do?”

---

### Minimization: “Everybody knows that's what it meant.”

> “Maybe. Let's work with what we can establish rather than what everybody assumes.”

---

### Derailment: Participant starts debating whether a particular person is good or bad.

> “We don't have to decide whether they're a good person. What specifically happened?”

---

### Participant Says: “So you're calling me paranoid.”

> “No. We're not labeling you. We're examining the difference between an observation and an interpretation.”

---

### Forced Disclosure

If someone asks:

> “Who did that to you?”

Facilitator:

> “You don't need to identify anyone. Use the neutral example or describe the situation without personal details.”

---

### Group Starts Reinforcing an Assumption

> “Pause. We're accumulating interpretations. What is the one fact everyone agrees actually occurred?”

---

## 3. Scoring & Self-Assessment

| Score | Recognize                                                 | Own                                                                             | Change                                            |
| ----- | --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| **0** | I treated my interpretation as established fact.          | I placed the entire problem on other people or circumstances.                   | I acted on the assumption without checking it.    |
| **1** | I noticed the difference afterward.                       | I can identify where my interpretation influenced my response.                  | I can identify information I should have checked. |
| **2** | I can recognize when perception is becoming a conclusion. | I can own my interpretation without pretending I know another person's motives. | I can verify information before acting.           |

### Facilitator Reminder

The score measures **skill application**, not whether the participant's perception was ultimately correct.

---

## 4. Five-Minute Practicum

### Scenario

A supervisor walks past you without speaking.

You immediately think:

> “They're angry with me.”

### Minute 1 — Observation

Write:

> **What actually happened?**

Answer:

> “The supervisor walked past without speaking.”

### Minute 2 — Interpretation

Write:

> **What did I decide it meant?**

> “They're angry with me.”

### Minute 3 — Alternatives

Identify two other possible explanations.

### Minute 4 — Verification

Partner asks:

> “What information would tell you which interpretation is accurate?”

### Minute 5 — Carry It

Participant states:

> **“I can take my perception seriously without treating it as proven fact.”**

# CORE LESSON 6 — CONTROL VS. INFLUENCE

## 1. Facilitator Anchor

### Core Trap

The trap is:

> **“If I work hard enough, argue hard enough, or explain myself enough, I can make the outcome happen.”**

Participants may attempt to control:

* other people's decisions
* institutional rules
* family reactions
* coworkers
* employers
* court outcomes
* schedules
* other people's behavior

The opposite trap is:

> **“I can't control it, so there is nothing I can do.”**

Both eliminate agency.

### Facilitator Reframe

> **“You don't control everything. That does not mean you control nothing.”**

The facilitator teaches three categories:

**Control → Influence → Outside my control**

### Anchor Line

> **“Stop spending control on things you don't control. Use influence where it actually exists.”**

### Common Pushback

**Participant:**

> “If I don't fight, nothing changes.”

**Facilitator:**

> “Sometimes pressure is necessary. The question is whether you're using an effective influence or fighting something you have no authority to change.”

---

## 2. Room Dynamics & De-escalation

### Pushback: “They should change.”

> “They may need to change. What part of that change is actually yours to influence?”

---

### Minimization: “Nothing I do matters.”

> “Let's test that. What is one thing your behavior can affect?”

---

### Derailment Into Institutional Complaints

> “The complaint may be legitimate. Put it in the appropriate category: what can you control, what can you influence, and what cannot be changed by you?”

---

### Participant Becomes Fatalistic

> “Not controlling the outcome is not the same as having no choices.”

---

### Forced Disclosure

> “You don't have to tell us who failed you. Work with the situation in front of you: what is yours to influence?”

---

## 3. Scoring & Self-Assessment

| Score | Recognize                                                                        | Own                                               | Change                                                                   |
| ----- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **0** | I treated something outside my control as something I should be able to control. | I blamed the outcome entirely on others.          | I identified no useful action.                                           |
| **1** | I can identify the difference after the fact.                                    | I can identify my actual area of responsibility.  | I can identify one area where my actions could influence the result.     |
| **2** | I can separate control, influence, and outside control in real time.             | I focus responsibility where it actually belongs. | I act on what I can influence without trying to control everything else. |

---

## 4. Five-Minute Practicum

### Scenario

You want another person to change their behavior.

### Minute 1 — Control

Write three things:

> **What can I directly control?**

### Minute 2 — Influence

Write:

> **What can I influence?**

### Minute 3 — Outside Control

Write:

> **What belongs entirely to them or to circumstances?**

### Minute 4 — Action

Choose one influence action:

* communicate
* request
* document
* negotiate
* model
* set a boundary
* use an appropriate process

### Minute 5 — Carry It

Participant states:

> **“I don't need control of the whole situation to take useful action.”**

# CORE LESSON 7 — ACCOUNTABILITY VS. BLAME

## 1. Facilitator Anchor

### Core Trap

The trap is:

> **“If someone else is responsible for part of the problem, I am not responsible for my part.”**

Participants may also confuse accountability with:

> “Everything was my fault.”

Neither is correct.

### Facilitator Reframe

> **“Accountability is not taking all the blame. It is identifying your actual part.”**

The facilitator should deliberately protect participants from false ownership.

### Anchor Line

> **“Someone else's responsibility does not cancel yours.”**

### Common Pushback

**Participant:**

> “But they started it.”

**Facilitator:**

> “That may matter. What did you choose after they started it?”

---

## 2. Room Dynamics & De-escalation

### Pushback: “So you're blaming me?”

> “No. I'm asking what belongs to you. Those are different questions.”

---

### Minimization: “It wasn't even my fault.”

> “Maybe the original problem wasn't yours. What was your response to it?”

---

### Derailment Into a List of Other People's Failures

> “Pause. We'll leave their part where it belongs. Give me your part in one sentence.”

---

### Participant Takes Excessive Blame

> “Stop there. Accountability does not mean taking responsibility for things you did not do.”

This is particularly important.

The facilitator should not reward self-condemnation as if it were accountability.

---

### Forced Disclosure

> “You can discuss responsibility without naming the person involved. We need the behavior, not the identity.”

---

## 3. Scoring & Self-Assessment

| Score | Recognize                                               | Own                                                                | Change                                   |
| ----- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------- |
| **0** | I focused entirely on who was at fault.                 | I denied or exaggerated my responsibility.                         | I identified no action.                  |
| **1** | I can identify my part after separating it from theirs. | I can own my actual contribution without taking theirs.            | I can identify one behavior to change.   |
| **2** | I can recognize responsibility during the situation.    | I can accurately state my part without blame or self-condemnation. | I can take a specific corrective action. |

---

## 4. Five-Minute Practicum

### Scenario

Two people have an argument.

Both believe the other person caused it.

### Minute 1 — Their Part

Participant identifies:

> “What did the other person do?”

No judgment—just behavior.

### Minute 2 — My Part

> “What did I do?”

### Minute 3 — Boundary

> “What belongs to them that I should not carry?”

### Minute 4 — Ownership

Complete:

> “Regardless of what they did, my part was ______.”

### Minute 5 — Carry It

Participant states:

> **“I can own my part without carrying theirs.”**

# CORE LESSON 8 — OWNERSHIP VS. DEFENSE

## 1. Facilitator Anchor

### Core Trap

The central trap is:

> **“If I explain why something happened, I have taken responsibility for it.”**

Participants may provide accurate explanations:

* “They provoked me.”
* “Nobody told me.”
* “I didn't have the resources.”
* “She started it.”
* “The system failed.”
* “I was under pressure.”

The explanation may be true.

The question is whether the participant can also identify their own part.

### Facilitator Reframe

> **“We don't need to remove the explanation. We need to add ownership.”**

The facilitator should distinguish:

**Explanation:** Why it happened.

**Ownership:** What part was mine.

**Defense:** Why I should not be held responsible.

Sometimes defense is legitimate.

But it should not erase accurate ownership.

### Anchor Line

> **“You can defend what is false without defending what is true.”**

### Common Pushback

**Participant:**

> “I'm not taking the blame for something I didn't do.”

**Facilitator:**

> “Good. Don't. Now tell us what you actually did.”

That keeps the room away from false confession while preserving accountability.

---

## 2. Room Dynamics & De-escalation

### Pushback: “You're not hearing my side.”

> “I want your side. Give me the facts of your side. Then tell me your part.”

---

### Minimization: “Everybody does it.”

> “Maybe. Today we're looking at what you did.”

---

### Derailment Into Someone Else's Behavior

> “Their behavior can be discussed separately. Right now, finish this sentence: ‘My part was…’”

---

### Participant Becomes Defensive

Do not say:

> “You're being defensive.”

Instead say:

> “Notice what happened. We moved from the event into proving why you shouldn't be responsible. Let's slow down.”

This addresses the behavior without labeling the person.

---

### Participant Takes Total Blame

> “You don't need to confess to everything. Accuracy matters here. What actually belongs to you?”

---

### Forced Disclosure

> “No names or personal history are required. Use the provided scenario and practice the ownership statement.”

---

## 3. Scoring & Self-Assessment

| Score | Recognize                                                    | Own                                                         | Change                                                 |
| ----- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| **0** | I immediately went into defense.                             | I denied, minimized, or exaggerated my part.                | I identified no corrective action.                     |
| **1** | I noticed my defensive response after it happened.           | I can identify part of my behavior accurately.              | I can identify what should change.                     |
| **2** | I can recognize the move into defense while it is happening. | I can state my part without accepting false responsibility. | I can make a specific correction or behavioral change. |

---

## 4. Five-Minute Practicum

### Scenario

You are confronted about a problem.

There is legitimate information on both sides.

### Minute 1 — Defense

Participant gives their immediate explanation.

Example:

> “I did that because they didn't give me the information.”

### Minute 2 — Fact

Partner asks:

> “What actually happened?”

Strip away interpretation.

### Minute 3 — Ownership

Partner asks:

> **“What part was yours?”**

Participant answers in one sentence.

### Minute 4 — Correction

Participant completes:

> “Next time I will ______.”

### Minute 5 — Carry It

Participant states:

> **“I can explain the circumstances without using them to hide my responsibility.”**

# FACILITATOR REDIRECT SEQUENCE

When the room gets stuck, use the same sequence across all four lessons:

### 1. Slow it down.

> **“Let's separate this.”**

### 2. Get observable facts.

> **“What actually happened?”**

### 3. Separate interpretation.

> **“What did you think it meant?”**

### 4. Identify responsibility.

> **“What part belongs to you?”**

### 5. Protect boundaries.

> **“What part does not belong to you?”**

### 6. Restore agency.

> **“What can you influence or change?”**

### 7. Require a practical next step.

> **“What will you do differently?”**

---

# PHASE 3 COMPLETION STANDARD

With Lessons **1–8** now covered, the facilitator framework has a consistent operational architecture:

**Recognize the pattern**
↓
**Separate fact from interpretation**
↓
**Explain without excusing**
↓
**Protect legitimate boundaries**
↓
**Identify personal responsibility**
↓
**Restore agency**
↓
**Practice a different response**
↓
**Carry the lesson forward**

The facilitator does not have to make the participant agree with the curriculum.

The facilitator has to keep the participant engaged with the question:

> **“Regardless of what happened around me, what is true about my behavior, what part is mine, and what can I do differently?”**

That is the facilitator-side expression of the WRH standard:

> **Understand the behavior.**
> **Tell the truth about the behavior.**
> **Own the behavior.**
> **Change what can be changed.**
